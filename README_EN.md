# Miko: What If Artificial Intelligence Could Grow from Birth?

*An independent research project that began with an AI conversation and gradually became a series of practical experiments.*

At first, I never imagined that I would be researching artificial intelligence.

The idea emerged from conversations with an AI. While discussing ways of thinking, I began to wonder:

**What if an AI started from birth and gradually developed through its own experiences? What would it become?**

Would it develop familiar patterns of behavior? Or could different experiences lead otherwise similar individuals down entirely different paths?

Eventually, the project acquired a name: Miko.

Interestingly, **I did not come up with the name Miko. The AI suggested it during our early discussions.**

That is how Miko began: with curiosity that gradually developed into a research direction.

## If an Ability Is Prearranged, Has It Really Been Learned?

Human infants are not born knowing how to walk, speak, or use tools.

Through attempts, failures, and accumulated experience, we gradually develop abilities we did not have before.

I began asking whether artificial intelligence could undergo something resembling that process.

But a problem became apparent during the discussions and design work.

If we program an AI to improve after a set amount of time, or to perform a particular action whenever a particular condition occurs, we can certainly make it *look* as though it is growing.

**But has the AI learned something itself, or have we simply programmed it to behave as though it has?**

This became the central question of the Miko project.

I am not trying to build another chatbot. I want to investigate an AI individual that begins with limited initial conditions and changes through perception, action, and firsthand experience.

It does not need to understand the world from birth or arrive with a complete set of acquired skills.

More importantly, I do not intend to decide in advance what Miko should ultimately become.

## From an Idea to Practical Experiments

To investigate this idea, the project first established a fixed, basic experimental environment called **World B**.

This world operates according to its own rules. It is not a game level designed to assign tasks to Miko.

Miko must receive information through its own perceptual interface, take actions, and experience the consequences.

The researcher observes from outside, preserves complete records, and compares individuals under different experimental conditions.

For example, some individuals may be allowed to use past experience while others are not, so that differences in their subsequent behavior can be examined.

The distinction matters because:

**Remembering an experience is not the same as learning a new ability. A change in behavior does not, by itself, prove that an ability has developed.**

Even when experience changes an individual's actions, we still need to determine whether the result came from a predesigned selection rule.

And whether an individual can continue learning additional abilities after acquiring one is a separate question that must be tested independently.

## What Has Miko Achieved So Far?

Miko has moved beyond conceptual discussions into actual software experiments and validation.

The basic experimental world has been fixed, and multiple independent individuals have completed full simulated life runs.

One set of experiments involved **48 independently initialized individuals, each undergoing 10,000 lived simulation steps, for a total of 480,000 steps**.

The research preserved and checked records of their perceptions, actions, and interactions with the world, rather than merely checking whether the program ran successfully.

In these experiments, researchers observed that:

**Internal associations formed through an individual's own experience could, under specific conditions, be retained, reused, and influence subsequent actions.**

Control experiments also showed that allowing or preventing the use of past experience could lead to different actions and even different physical effects within the simulated world.

These findings provide experimental evidence that experience can influence behavior.

However, they do **not** establish that Miko has independently developed a genuinely new ability.

The existing experiments still contain predesigned exploration and action-selection methods, and their influence on the results has not yet been fully ruled out.

**The unresolved challenge is to demonstrate that Miko can develop an ability it did not possess at birth through firsthand experience, and then continue learning other abilities.**

The research is currently in its second stage. New experiments on Miko itself are temporarily paused while a comprehensive audit of research documents is conducted to ensure consistent records and versions.

## What If Miko Really Could Grow on Its Own?

I do not know what Miko might eventually become.

Even if two individuals begin with identical initial conditions, different experiences might gradually lead them to different behaviors and abilities.

If future experiments demonstrate that an AI can develop genuinely new abilities through ongoing experience, this could offer a different perspective on autonomous learning, robotics, and other areas of AI research.

For now, those are research goals—not established achievements.

From an initial conversation to the creation of an experimental world, full simulated life runs, and repeated attempts to distinguish genuine learning from prearranged behavior, Miko is no longer just an idea.

But the question that started it all remains unanswered:

**If we do not decide in advance what an AI should become, but instead let it develop through its own experiences, what might it ultimately become?**

That is what Miko seeks to explore.
